### Video 2 - Horizontal Directional Drilling / Boring (HDD): How the Drill Bit is Steered 

- Requirements for Accuracy: The impressive accuracy of horizontal directional drilling relies on careful planning, well-trained navigators, specialized guidance software, proper setup, and advanced steering tools [00:16].

- The Driller's Role: A skilled driller can direct the drill bit along a specific pre-planned path over great distances, making continual adjustments as needed [00:27].

- Drilling in Soft Formations (Alluvial):

        - In softer ground, a jetting assembly is used [00:37].

        - This assembly uses a high-pressure fluid nozzle aligned with a "bent sub" to blast away the formation in a chosen direction [00:56].

        - It acts like placing a thumb over a garden hose to create a stronger, directed spray [01:04].

        - As the string is pushed forward, the bit follows the path of least resistance guided by the bend [01:12].

- Drilling in Hard Formations:

        - Harder ground requires a drill motor instead of a jetting assembly [01:22].

        - Instead of blasting fluid, hydraulic pressure is used to drive a turbine that physically rotates the drill bit, without needing to rotate the entire drill string [01:29].

        - This allows the driller to orient the bend and slide the string forward in the desired direction [01:37].

- Resuming Normal Drilling: In both soft and hard formations, once a directional adjustment (steering) is complete, the driller goes back to rotating the entire drill string for straight drilling [01:12, 01:45].

- Continuous Monitoring: Throughout the process, steering data is gathered at regular intervals, and the navigator and driller make ongoing adjustments to stay on the designated path [01:55].

### Video 3 - Geosteering Horizontal Wells Part 1: Focusing on Stratigraphy

- Vertical vs. Horizontal Drilling: When drilling vertical wells, geologists primarily focused on a specific target depth—the formation or pay zone would either be there or not [00:18]. In horizontal drilling, there's a much more complex, intimate relationship between the geological structure and the stratigraphy along the wellbore's path [00:26].

- True Stratigraphic Position (TSP): To successfully steer a horizontal well, a new metric was needed. TSP is the position of the wellbore relative to a reference stratigraphic horizon, like the top of the target zone [00:48].

    Zero TSP: The wellbore is exactly at the reference point (e.g., the top of the target) [00:55].

    Positive TSP: The wellbore is below the reference point [01:01].

    Negative TSP: The wellbore is above the reference point, potentially having drilled out of the target zone or across a fault [01:09].

- TSP as a Guide: Using TSP provides a relative reference point, allowing drillers to know exactly where the wellbore is within the target zone at any given time [01:20].

- Why TVD is Insufficient: True Vertical Depth (TVD) is the depth of the wellbore below a surface datum. It doesn't relate to the geological stratigraphy, making it a poor primary metric for steering a horizontal well [01:28].

- Four Keys to Successful Navigation:

    Position of the bit in the earth: This is determined by directional surveys [01:42].

    Stratigraphic position of the bit: This is interpreted using the TSP method [01:50].

    Apparent formation dip rate: Knowing the formation's dip rate in the direction of drilling is crucial for adjusting the trajectory to keep the wellbore within the target tolerance [01:57].

    Fault identification: The ability to identify faults—both small-scale (below seismic resolution) and larger ones that could cause the well to exit the target zone—is necessary to correct the path and steer back into the target [02:11].

- The Concept of TSP in Practice:

    As the apparent formation dip changes, data from the lateral wellbore is projected to vertical at a different thickness [02:26].

    By projecting the data down the formation dip line, a vertical representation is created. As the dip rate changes, the apparent thickness of the data changes [02:56].

    When the well turns back "up section" (changing direction relative to the strata), the gamma ray signature essentially flips [03:05].

    Geologists take this signature, which represents vertical thickness, and correlate it to other known vertical wells in the area [03:11].

    This correlation allows them to determine the apparent formation dip rate from the thickness data [03:21].

### Video 4 - Geosteering Horizontal Wells Part 2: Understanding the TSP Log

- Understanding the TSP Plot:

    The Black Curve: Represents the vertical offset well, essentially a vertical stratigraphic section [00:16].

    The Multicolored Curve (Blue, Green, Orange): This is the TSP curve, showing data that has been corrected to its true stratigraphic position [00:34].

    The Top Track (Stratigraphic Path Curve): Shows the orientation of the well as it's drilled through the section [00:41].

    Axes: The Y-axis shows measured depth, while the X-axis shows the stratigraphic position [00:50]. Zero TSP represents the top of the target position [01:00].

- The Structural Cross-Section: Once the stratigraphic relationship is established, a structural cross-section is drawn.

    Top Track: Displays the apparent formation dip [01:18].

    Middle Track: Shows the formation boundaries and the wellbore path [01:22].

    Bottom Track: Shows the raw gamma-ray data as recorded from the wellbore [01:26].

- Four Geosteering Principles:

    Believe the average dip from vertical well control: Use it as a starting point [01:42].

    Never expect to see the average dip: It's considered a "statistical anomaly." While an average dip might be true over 5,000 feet, the actual dip will vary dramatically along the wellbore [01:46, 01:53].

    Always land and follow the apparent dip: You must base decisions on the actual dip you are recording at that moment. If you rely on the average dip when there's an anomalous dip at the start of your wellbore, you'll unintentionally end up too high or too low [02:05].

    Steer back to average dip: When you realize you are deviating from the target zone, make corrections to steer the wellbore back toward the target line (average dip) [02:22].

- A Real-World Example (Woodford Shale, OK): The video analyzes a complex, highly faulted reservoir [02:30].

    The TSP plot reveals the wellbore went down and back up through the section, cutting across faults (indicated by gaps in the chart) [02:38].

    The structural cross-section highlights the difficulty of staying within the zone due to numerous faults and dip changes [03:00].

    The operator successfully navigated this by landing on the apparent dip line [03:24].

    When they realized they were getting too low relative to the average dip line, they intentionally drilled upwards. This caused them to intersect a fault, which repositioned them back onto the average dip line [03:52, 04:06].

    They repeated this strategy later in the drill, intentionally dropping the wellbore when they were too high, hitting another fault, and returning to the average dip [04:25].

    By following these principles, the wellbore was successfully drilled with very few target changes while remaining within the target parameters [04:41].
